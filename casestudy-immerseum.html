An AR museum guide system delivered through Meta Quest 3. Designed to transform passive exhibit viewing into personalised, multilingual, sensory storytelling — so every visitor leaves having experienced something unique to them.

01 · The Spark

When a museum visit raises a design question

"What if immersive technology could make museums more personal, engaging, and easier to navigate?"

When I first visited a museum in the UK, I was overwhelmed. The building was full of history and culture, but I didn't know where to start. I wandered randomly, reading plaques here and there — and by the end, I couldn't recall most of what I had seen.

That experience raised a question that became the foundation of this project, designed for my Master's module in Advanced and Immersive Technologies. The goal wasn't to build AR for its own sake — it was to ask whether immersive technology could solve a real, felt problem in one of our most important cultural institutions.

02 · Research

Understanding the challenge

We began with open research, gathering insights into how people currently experience museums — and how museums are beginning to integrate technology.

Problem 01
Visitors face information overload — too much text, too little storytelling. Plaques are passive; they don't adapt to what a visitor already knows or cares about.
Problem 02
Navigation feels confusing and random, especially for first-time visitors. Without a clear route, people miss key exhibits or leave feeling disoriented.
Problem 03
The story behind exhibits is often lost in translation — reducing engagement and learning, particularly for non-native speakers or visitors with different learning styles.
The Gap
While the British Museum, Smithsonian, and National Museum of Singapore all use AR or digital guides, most solutions lack personalisation and adaptive storytelling.

04 · Scenarios

What it actually feels like to use Immerseum

Before sketching a single screen, we wrote detailed usage scenarios for each persona. This forced us to think in experience rather than features — to ask not "what does this button do" but "what is this person feeling right now, and what do they need next."

A
Anna's Journey
Museum visit · selects Spanish on Meta Quest 3
Anna visits a museum and selects Spanish as her language on the AR headset. She receives spatial audio narration with optional subtitles, tailored to her interests in ancient architecture and mythology. The system suggests a personalised route and uses subtle visual markers with haptic feedback to guide her — ensuring she explores confidently without feeling lost. She never has to read a plaque. The experience makes her visit independent, engaging, and culturally meaningful.
D
David's Journey
Birmingham Museum & Art Gallery · Meta Quest 3 + haptic gloves
David uses his Meta Quest 3 and museum-provided haptic gloves. He sets English narration, medium pace, and selects virtual guide "Theo." AR navigation arrows lead him to the Staffordshire Hoard. Theo appears as a 3D projection and brings history to life with visuals and storytelling. David interacts with objects through zoomable overlays and 3D models — even feeling textures using haptic gloves. The AR tour turns a typical museum visit into a hands-on, memorable learning adventure.
L
Leo's Journey
Museum visit · avoids crowded spaces
Leo uses the AR headset to personalise his tour before he even enters the main galleries. Calm navigation markers guide him directly, while a virtual avatar explains exhibits through his headphones — blocking background noise from other visitors. Familiar with VR, he finds the AR features intuitive. He explores at his own pace with full control. The experience gives him confidence, quiet enjoyment, and ends with a personalised summary of what he learned — something he can revisit at home.
05 · System Architecture

The technical backbone

After completing personas and scenarios, the next task was mapping the flow of data and devices. The architecture needed to support real-time personalisation, multilingual narration, and haptic feedback — simultaneously, in a physical museum space.

1
User Device — Meta Quest 3 with depth sensor, IMU, and hand tracking. The primary interface for all AR overlays, navigation, and interaction.
2
Unity Engine + Meta SDK — handles AR rendering, tour guide logic, spatial metadata, and AI/NLP Q&A. The brain of the experience.
3
Haptic Gloves API (Manus SDK) — enables texture simulation and physical interaction with 3D artefact models. Allows users like David to feel history.
4
Cloud Storage — artefact information, exhibit stories, and tour options. Content management system for personalised route generation.
5
IoT Sensors + Beacons — spatial mapping for exhibit recognition. Depth sensors and spatial anchors trigger contextual content as users move through the museum.

06 · Design Concept

Five features that make the experience

The solution was a customisable AR tour system — built on five core capabilities that each address a specific research finding and persona need. No feature was added without mapping back to a user problem.

Anna · Leo · David
Adaptive Storytelling
AR overlays, historical reconstructions, and contextual narration that changes based on visitor interest, knowledge level, and language — not a fixed script.
Anna · Leo
Personalised Tours
Language, themes, and pacing tailored to the visitor before they enter. Tour preferences set upfront ensure every journey is unique from the first step.
Anna · Leo · David
Intuitive Navigation
AR arrows overlaid on the physical floor and haptic cues guide visitors without breaking immersion. Distance to next exhibit displayed in real time.
Anna · Leo
Accessibility First
Subtitles, audio descriptions in multiple languages, and reduced transitions for sensory sensitivity. Designed to include, not accommodate.
David
Interactive Exhibits
Gesture-based interactions, 3D artefact models, zoomable overlays, and haptic texture simulation — turning glass cases into hands-on experiences.
All personas
Virtual Tour Guides
Selectable 3D avatar guides who appear as holographic projections alongside exhibits, narrate stories, and answer questions in real time via AI/NLP.

08 · Iteration

What testing changed

Qualitative feedback surfaced two specific interface failures — both related to AR spatial UI conventions that don't yet have established patterns. The fixes were targeted and grounded directly in what users described.

Before
Navigation arrows: Arrows could be more distinctive and participants wanted to know how far away the next exhibit was. The arrows blended into the environment at distance.
After
Sharper arrows with higher visual contrast, plus live distance text ("← 0 ft") added to the arrow — so visitors always know exactly how far they are from the next point of interest.
Before
Exhibit selector: No indication that scrolling was possible on the "Choose an Exhibit" screen. Users missed exhibits that were below the fold, not realising the list continued.
After
Added a gesture animation directing the user how to scroll — a visual cue that respects the spatial UI paradigm and teaches the interaction pattern without relying on text.

09 · Outcomes & Looking Ahead

What Immerseum proved

"This project taught me how immersive technology can create inclusive, personalised storytelling in cultural spaces. It was not just about building an AR system — it was about designing experiences that respect users' diverse needs, and making museums more human, one story at a time."

What each persona gained

A
For Anna
Access in her language
AR isn't just a visual enhancement for Anna — it's linguistic access. Spatial audio narration in Spanish means she can engage deeply with exhibits that would otherwise feel distant. Immerseum makes cultural heritage available to people who've been excluded by language barriers in physical institutions.
D
For David
A playful, interactive way to learn
Museums become genuinely engaging for a generation that expects interactivity. Being able to touch a 3D reconstruction of an artefact changes the relationship between visitor and history — from passive observation to active participation.
L
For Leo
Calm, structured exploration without stress
AR creates a private bubble in a public space — headphones blocking background noise, calm navigation markers reducing decision fatigue. Leo can experience culture without the anxiety that has historically kept him away from it.

Where this goes next

🤖
AI-Powered Curation
Dynamic curation that adapts in real time to visitor behaviour — suggesting exhibits based on how long they linger, what they interact with, and questions they ask.
👓
AR Smart Glasses
As form factor shrinks, smart glasses with real-time translation could make every museum visit multilingual by default — no headset required.
🎵
Multi-Sensory Experiences
Combining AR with spatial soundscapes and haptic feedback at scale — full environmental storytelling where the museum literally comes alive around the visitor.

Reflection

Designing for AR introduced constraints I hadn't encountered in screen-based work. There are no established pattern libraries for XR navigation, no widely understood conventions for AR overlays in public spaces. Every interaction had to be either self-evidently intuitive or explicitly taught — and the testing showed exactly where the balance hadn't been found.

The most valuable lesson was about designing for diverse access rather than diverse preference. Anna, David, and Leo don't just want different things — they need different things to be able to participate at all. AR technology, when designed thoughtfully, can close that gap in ways that physical museum design simply cannot.

Museums are one of our last genuinely public spaces — places that exist to share human knowledge with every human. Immerseum was a proof of concept that they don't have to feel the same for everyone in order to feel meaningful to everyone.
