YuSign — Designing an accessible way to learn sign language

A mobile-first BSL learning app designed from day one for users with low vision, deafness, motor impairment, and ADHD. Accessibility wasn't a feature added at the end. It was the foundation.

01 · Overview

The problem worth solving

Millions of Deaf and hard-of-hearing people face communication barriers every day. At the same time, hearing people who want to learn sign language find existing tools inaccessible, jargon-heavy, or overwhelming.

Most sign language learning apps are built for hearing users without disabilities, then retrofitted with accessibility as an afterthought. YuSign set out to flip that assumption — designing a BSL learning app where inclusivity shapes the product from day one, not the end.

"What if accessibility shaped the product from day one, rather than being bolted on at the end?"

02 · Research

Starting with people, not assumptions

Rather than guessing who would use YuSign, I designed four accessibility personas to guide every design decision. Each persona represented a real set of needs grounded in disability research and WCAG 2.2 success criteria.

Aisha · Low Vision
Age 33 · Student · Birmingham, UK

Uses high-contrast mode and zooms to 200%. Finds cluttered interfaces frustrating. Needs scalable, legible content that survives magnification.

"Faint text means I miss instructions entirely."

Priya · Cerebral Palsy
Age 26 · Illustrator · London, UK

Fine motor control affected. Small buttons and precision gestures are frustrating. Needs large tap targets, generous spacing, and switch control support.

"Shaky motor control means small buttons are punishing."

Mateo · Deaf
Age 21 · Student · London, UK

Communicates primarily through BSL. Apps that rely on audio without visual alternatives don't work for him. Needs captions, text labels, and visual feedback.

"Audio-only feedback excludes me completely."

Ben · ADHD & Dyslexia
Age 28 · Project Manager · Manchester, UK

Sensitive to information overload. Needs uncluttered layouts, predictable navigation, and chunked content to stay focused.

"Unpredictable layouts increase my cognitive load instantly."

These four personas became the North Star. Every screen had to work for all of them — if a decision helped Aisha but broke the experience for Ben, it wasn't good enough.

03 · Ideation

Paper first, pixels second

I started with paper sketches — messy, fast, and honest. The goal was to explore how a user would learn a sign, replay it, bookmark it, take a quiz, and track progress without committing to a visual direction too early.

This approach surfaced problems early. Quiz layouts with multiple answer options looked intimidating for Ben. The sketching phase led directly to large, spaced cards with a single answer per view and immediate visual feedback.
04 · Design

Designing the experience

Onboarding

One question per screen. No walls of text, no overwhelming choices. Users tell the app why they're learning BSL, personalising their experience. Returning users skip directly to home.

Homepage & Learn Screen

Large cards, high contrast, and consistent bottom navigation. The Learn screen features sign videos with captions, replay controls, a slow-sign option, and text descriptions. Every interaction has visual and textual feedback — no audio-only cues.

Quiz

Large cards, instant "Correct! Well done!" feedback, and a clear next action. Four quiz formats (text, image, matching) keep engagement varied without overwhelming users.

Bookmarks & Search

Confirmation modals prevent accidental actions — a direct response to Priya's motor impairment and Ben's tendency to mis-tap. Search supports predictive results by category.

05 · Accessibility

WCAG 2.2 — every decision mapped back

Every design choice was mapped back to a WCAG 2.2 criterion and one of the four personas. Accessibility wasn't a checklist at the end — it was the design rationale throughout.

1.4.6 · AAA — Contrast Enhanced
All text achieves at least 7:1 contrast. For Aisha (low vision), faint text could mean missing instructions entirely. Verified with Figma contrast plugin on every screen.
2.5.5 · AAA — Target Size Enhanced
All buttons and cards at minimum 44×44px. For Priya (cerebral palsy), larger tap areas made the app forgiving of shaky motor control.
1.2.2 · AA — Captions
All sign videos include text labels and captions. For Mateo (Deaf), he could follow lessons independently without relying on audio.
3.2.3 · AA — Consistent Navigation
Bottom nav placed identically across all screens. For Ben (ADHD/dyslexia), consistency reduced disorientation and helped him build a mental map of the app quickly.
2.4.7 · AA — Focus Visible
All interactive elements show a clear focus ring. For Priya, using a switch device required visible, predictable focus management throughout the entire flow.
1.3.3 · A — Sensory Characteristics
Instructions never rely on shape, colour, or position alone. For Mateo and Aisha, every affordance has a text equivalent or label so nothing is missed.
06 · Scaling

One product, every device

Once the mobile flow was solid, YuSign was adapted for iPad and desktop. The design remained consistent with no surprises for users switching devices.

Mobile
Single-column, large tap targets, portrait-first
Tablet
Larger grids, increased spacing, landscape-friendly
Desktop
Keyboard focus rings, tab order, expanded content area
07 · Testing

Testing with real people

YuSign was tested with five participants, none with prior BSL experience. Each completed tasks covering onboarding, learning a sign, bookmarking, taking a quiz, searching, and checking progress. After tasks, participants rated the app using the System Usability Scale (SUS).

Onboarding
Create account and complete the BSL level assessment. All 5 participants completed without assistance.
Learn a sign
Navigate to and watch a lesson video to completion. 5/5 success — most used the repeat feature unprompted.
Bookmark and quiz
Save a sign and take a quiz on recently learned content. 4/5 completed; one user initially missed the bookmark icon.
Search and progress
Use search to find a specific sign and locate the progress dashboard. 5/5 completed — progress view was described as motivating.

08 · Iteration

What didn't work — and how I fixed it

On the onboarding screen, 3 out of 5 participants confused the summary boxes for tappable buttons and tapped them instead of the real CTA. A clear affordance problem.

The problem

Summary feature boxes on "Let's Get Started" looked like tappable cards. Users expected them to do something — missing the actual "Next" button.

I converted the summary boxes into plain content blocks without card-style borders, and made the "Next" button the single unmistakably primary interactive element.

09 · Impact

What YuSign proved

"Designing inclusively from the start is both practical and powerful."

YuSign demonstrated that accessibility-first design doesn't mean compromising on quality — it means building something that genuinely works for more people.

Accessible

Reflection

This project reinforced that accessibility isn't just a checklist. It's about empathy, anticipation, and designing for real human diversity. By focusing on people first, YuSign became more than a sign language app — it became a proof of concept for what inclusive design can achieve.

The most meaningful validation came not from the SUS score, but from the participant with ADHD who said the uncluttered design helped them focus. That's the kind of outcome that matters.
