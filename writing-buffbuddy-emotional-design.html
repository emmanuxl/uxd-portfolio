When the problem is emotional, the solution has to be too

Emmanuel Edigbe · 2025

There is a particular kind of design problem that cannot be solved with better information architecture. You can reorganise the navigation, refine the onboarding flow, and optimise every tap target — and the problem will remain, because the problem is not in the interface. It is in how the user feels before they even open the app.

BuffBuddy was exactly that kind of problem. The brief was to design a fitness app for gym beginners. But the real brief — the one that emerged from research — was something more specific: design an app for people who want to go to the gym but are afraid to.

Gym intimidation is a documented psychological phenomenon. It is the insecurity, the fear of judgement, the anxiety of not knowing what to do in a space that seems to belong to people who already know exactly what they are doing. For a significant portion of beginners, this fear is the primary barrier to exercise — not access, not time, not equipment. The feeling.

What the research pointed to

The research on gym intimidation — the insecurity, anxiety and fear of judgement that makes exercising in public spaces overwhelming — pointed to something that no feature list would fix. Two personas made it concrete. Jenna, a Masters student, wanted company and a more enjoyable experience but was put off by the campus gym. Jake, an undergraduate who had lost his support system after moving away, was overwhelmed by the equipment and needed structure and reassurance.

The problem was not "help me track my workouts." It was "help me feel like I belong here."

Those are structurally different problems. The first is a productivity tool. The second is an emotional design problem — and it narrowed the project to three areas where the research was clearest: lack of structure, low self-confidence, and low motivation.

Designing for the feeling, not just the function

A standard fitness app reaches for features: exercise libraries, rep counters, progress charts. BuffBuddy mapped a feature to each problem instead: auto-generated workouts for the lack of structure, a virtual hype buddy for low confidence and motivation, and achievable goals that unlock buddy cosmetics.

The buddy is the heart of it, so its personality was defined before it was designed: approachable, encouraging without being judgmental. That led to two decisions that look like product choices but are really emotional ones. Streaks were deliberately avoided, because they can leave users feeling discouraged. And live encouragement is opt-in, so people decide how much of the buddy they want. The check-in before a workout was built on the same idea: some days you feel strong and some days you do not, and both are valid reasons to show up.

What usability testing revealed

Two rounds of testing — guerilla and A/B testing with 25 people, then a usability study with 16 — shaped the product. The first round changed direction: the 50/50 split between fitness and a mindfulness soundscape did not appeal, so it was swapped for the cheer-buddy concept, and small labelled buttons were replaced with larger ones carrying both an icon and a label. The second round, measured with the Post-Study System Usability Questionnaire alongside task observation, led to four targeted redesigns, and the usability score moved from 60% to 85%.

The most instructive finding came from the live motivation toggle. It was a small switch in the top corner with a short label, and the misclick rate on it was 72.7% — nearly three in four taps landed somewhere other than the toggle. Most participants only found it on the sixth click or later, and several said it was unclear. The problem was not only discoverability: a setting-style control in a corner does not read as an invitation. The fix was positional and tonal. The toggle moved into its own labelled card beneath the timer with a plain-language description, and the primary action was relabelled from "Start Exercise" to "Begin Workout".

The second finding was about structure. Participants felt the Mindfulness tab was out of place — one said they thought it was a fitness app. Confidence tracking moved into Stats as a seven-day view, so it read as part of fitness progress rather than a separate product. By the end, the words participants used most to describe the app were "confident" and "supported."

When emotional design is the solution

The broader lesson from BuffBuddy is about the category of problem it represents. Emotional barriers are real UX problems — they are just harder to see in analytics because they appear as absence. The user who did not download your app. The session that ended at the second screen. The feature that was never activated.

Usability testing is good at surfacing the symptoms of emotional friction. It is less good at diagnosing the cause — because participants describe what they did, not what they felt while doing it. The insight that gym intimidation was emotional, not informational, did not come from a task completion rate. It came from reading the research on gym intimidation and building personas around it before a single screen existed.

If the problem is emotional, the solution has to be emotional too. Better information architecture will not make someone feel less afraid of being judged. A product that treats confidence as a design value — that builds reassurance into every screen, every piece of copy, every interaction — might.
