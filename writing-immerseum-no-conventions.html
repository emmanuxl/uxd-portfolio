Designing for a medium with no conventions — lessons from Immerseum

Emmanuel Edigbe · 2025

Every design discipline carries a body of inherited knowledge. Conventions accumulated over decades — conventions so embedded that most designers apply them without thinking. Buttons look pressable. Navigation sits at the top. Progress moves left to right. Form follows a known shape.

These conventions exist because they work. Familiarity is a design resource: when a user recognises a pattern, they do not have to learn it. You borrow that recognition for free, and the cognitive load of using your product drops accordingly.

Augmented reality, as a design medium, does not yet have this inheritance. The conventions are still forming. The pattern libraries are thin. The best practices are contested. When I designed Immerseum — an AR museum experience for Meta Quest 3 — the absence of established conventions was the most significant constraint on the project. It was also, eventually, the most interesting one.

The problem with no reference points

When you design for mobile, users arrive with thousands of hours of interaction already encoded. A swipe right means something. A floating action button has a known affordance. You can rely on that familiarity — not entirely, but meaningfully.

In AR, that resource barely exists. Meta Quest 3 users — particularly in a museum context, where many visitors would be first-time headset wearers — arrived without a reliable interaction vocabulary. What does it mean to "select" something when your hands are the cursor? How do you signal that an exhibit is interactive rather than simply part of the environment? Where does navigation live when there is no screen edge to anchor it?

These questions do not have established answers. You have to derive them from first principles, which means grounding every decision in a specific understanding of how humans behave in physical space — because that is the only pre-existing vocabulary a spatial interface can borrow from.

When there are no established UX patterns for a platform, every interaction has to be either self-evidently intuitive or explicitly taught. There is no middle ground where familiarity does the work.

Self-evidently intuitive: what that actually means

Self-evidently intuitive interactions are those that exploit existing human behaviour so naturally that no instruction is required. Reaching toward an object to inspect it. Stepping closer to read something. Looking at a point of interest to signal attention. These work because they map directly onto physical-world behaviour that every visitor already has, regardless of their technology familiarity.

Immerseum's primary interaction model leaned on gaze-based triggering for exhibit information. A visitor looking at an artefact for more than 1.5 seconds would see a subtle highlight appear, followed by an information panel at a comfortable reading distance. No button. No controller input. The trigger was the same behaviour any interested visitor would exhibit — sustained attention on a single point.

The 1.5 second threshold was not arbitrary. It was observed. In the first unguided testing session, participants were asked to walk through a prototype exhibit space without instruction. We measured how long they held their gaze on exhibit points that genuinely interested them versus points they glanced at in passing. 1.5 seconds separated the two behaviours consistently across participants. Below that threshold, panels appeared accidentally. Above it, they appeared at the moment a visitor was already asking the implicit question: what is this?

What has to be explicitly taught

Not every AR interaction can be self-evidently intuitive — and recognising which category an interaction falls into is one of the more important design judgements in a medium without conventions.

Immerseum's haptic navigation system required explicit teaching. The system used haptic pulses through the Quest 3 controllers to guide visitors directionally: a short pulse on the left indicated something interesting to the left, a double pulse indicated something behind. This was not an intuitive affordance. No amount of careful design would make an unfamiliar haptic pattern self-explanatory.

The instruction approach was a thirty-second interactive onboarding at the experience start. Not a tutorial video or a text wall — an interactive demonstration where visitors experienced a haptic pulse while simultaneously seeing a visual indicator pointing in the signalled direction. After two or three repetitions, the mapping was encoded. Post-experience testing showed that all participants correctly interpreted directional haptics by the midpoint of their visit. The explicit teaching had transferred into something that felt, by the end, almost intuitive.

Designing for the broadest possible visitor

A museum context imposes an unusual design constraint: the visitor demographic is deliberately broad. Children, elderly visitors, international tourists, people who have never worn a headset. Immerseum had to work for all of them — which meant every affordance had to be designed for the least familiar user first.

The test was simple: could a visitor who had never used AR navigate to an exhibit, trigger its information layer, access an audio guide, and find the exit experience without assistance? This became the standard against which every interaction was evaluated. If it failed for the least familiar user, it was redesigned.

Language selection was one of the more complex problems in this category. It needed to happen before the experience began, without assuming the visitor could read the interface language. The solution was a flag-based screen with audio samples — a visitor could tap a flag to hear a short spoken phrase in that language, confirming their selection before committing. No literacy requirement. No assumption of prior familiarity with the flag of every represented country. Just: hear a voice, recognise the language, proceed.

Multilingual support extended into the experience itself. Exhibit information panels were available in seven languages with a gaze-triggered language switcher — a small flag icon persistent at the edge of each panel. The captions designed for Deaf visitors, which rendered spoken audio guide content as text, were adopted by hearing visitors as well — particularly in the noisier gallery spaces where the audio guide competed with ambient sound.

What designing without conventions teaches you

The broader lesson from Immerseum is about the relationship between constraints and creativity in UX. Designing for a medium with no conventions forces a kind of first-principles thinking that established platforms do not. You cannot reach for a pattern library. You cannot ask "what does Instagram do here?" You have to ask: given what I know about human behaviour, attention, and space — what makes sense?

That question produces different answers than "what is the established pattern?" Sometimes worse answers, because you lack the accumulated knowledge that conventions represent. But sometimes significantly better ones, because you are reasoning from the actual problem rather than from inherited solutions to different problems.

AR is at a formative moment. The interaction conventions that will define spatial interfaces for the next decade are being established now — through commercial products, research, and projects like Immerseum. The designers working in this space are not just solving individual design problems. They are contributing to the pattern library that will eventually exist. That is an unusual and significant responsibility, and it is one that makes the absence of conventions feel less like a constraint and more like an invitation.
